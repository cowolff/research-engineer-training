Scenario-based engineering training

Maia

Cognitive Science teaches you to reason about systems long before you ever have to build one. Maia closes that gap: it puts you in front of realistic engineering situations — a stack to design, a broken deployment to diagnose, a colleague's plan to pull apart — and works through each one with you until you can defend your answer.

Built for Cognitive Science students becoming AI research engineers.

How a session works

One scenario, start to finish. Nothing here is a multiple-choice quiz: every answer you give is free text, and the whole thing is a conversation.

  1. It decides what to test — in plain code, not by asking a model

    A deterministic selector weights concepts you have never seen, concepts you missed last time, and concepts you were recently given a tutorial on, then draws two to four of them from a single topic. What you practise is never left to a model's whim, and difficulty ramps with your rolling score.

  2. A scenario is generated, with its artifacts

    It arrives as one of five shapes: an open architecture question, a plausible-but-flawed design to review, a forced trade-off to defend, a concept to explain in context, or a debugging exercise in which the model fabricates the evidence — an OOM kill in docker logs, nvidia-smi sitting at 0 % utilisation, a stack trace, a CrashLoopBackOff — and leaves you to work out what happened.

  3. The rubric is fixed before you answer

    Every scenario is stored together with the concepts it grades and which of them are essential. That list is written when the scenario is generated, so nothing about the marking can be quietly reshaped around whatever you happen to write.

  4. You work it as a conversation, and the questions are nudges

    Three replies by default. Each turn reacts to what you actually wrote, names the thing you got right or wrong, and then asks one question aimed at a rubric item you have not reached yet. It points at the evidence or the consequence and leaves the cause to you: the GPU is at 4 % while latency is 30 s — what would have to be true for both at once? The conversation closes early once the essentials are covered, rather than padding out the turn budget.

  5. The ledger records how you got there

    Covered on your own counts towards mastery. Covered only after a nudge counts as neither progress nor a gap — you got there with help, so the concept simply comes round again later. Never covered is a miss. And if the grading is wrong, every missed item has an I did cover this button that corrects the record.

  6. A tutorial appears once a gap is real

    Miss the same essential concept three times across three different scenarios and Maia writes a tutorial from your own missed attempts, then re-tests that concept later to check the gap actually closed. Tutorials are canonical per concept, so a cohort builds a shared library as it trains.

Four things it refuses to do

The design commitments that the rest of the tool is arranged around.

Grade you against a moving target

The rubric exists before your answer does, and is never revised in light of it.

Hand you the answer

A nudge names the area at most, never the cause. Even the final turn, which is allowed to be broad, stops short of saying it for you.

Leak the answer through the glossary

Beside the scenario sits a small chat for vocabulary alone — what p95 latency is, what a digest is. It is shown the scenario text and nothing else: no rubric, no target concepts, no transcript. There is nothing in it to give away, and what you ask it can neither earn you credit nor cost you any.

Cite a link it made up

Tutorials draw on a curated resource index compiled into the build. The model chooses from a shortlist by id and the app resolves ids to URLs at render time, so it never emits a URL and cannot invent one.

Where the name comes from

μαῖα
maia

μαῖα is Greek for midwife. In Plato's Theaetetus, Socrates describes his own method as exactly that: he claims no wisdom of his own to pass along, only the craft of helping someone deliver an understanding they were already carrying. Later readers named the technique after the image — maieutic, from μαιευτικός, “of midwifery.”

That is the mechanic this whole tool is built on. It does not tell you the answer and then ask you to recall it; it asks the question that makes you reach for it yourself, and it only counts a concept as learned when you are the one who said it. The single place it will answer you plainly is the glossary chat, and that is walled off from your grade for precisely the same reason.
